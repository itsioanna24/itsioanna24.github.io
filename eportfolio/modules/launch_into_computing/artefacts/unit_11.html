Unit 11: Future-Proofing IT Systems: Integrating Risk Management and Strategic IT Governance

A Resilient & Strategic
IT Framework

A combined IT governance and risk management plan for a global e-commerce company — integrating COBIT 2019, a cloud-based disaster recovery strategy, and compliance with GDPR, PCI DSS, and the ACM Code of Ethics.

Strategic Report

Risk & Strategic Opportunity

Every significant risk carries a corresponding strategic opportunity. For a global e-commerce company, cybersecurity is both the primary threat and the primary lever for competitive differentiation.

⚠️
Key Risk · Critical

Cybersecurity Breaches

A significant risk for a global e-commerce organisation is cybersecurity breaches, potentially leading to customer data exposure, financial loss, and reputational damage. The scale and cross-border nature of operations amplifies exposure — a single breach can trigger GDPR enforcement, PCI DSS penalties, and sustained loss of customer trust imultaneously.

🚀
Strategic Opportunity

AI-Driven Fraud Detection

Deploying AI-driven fraud detection and threat analysis tools can enhance transaction security and improve customer confidence in e-commerce environments. Security-as-competitive-advantage reframes the cost of compliance as an investment in brand differentiation — particularly relevant in markets where trust is a primary purchase driver.

Combined IT Governance & Risk Plan

The plan is structured around three interlocking pillars, each directly addressing one of the assignment's three requirements.

🏛️
Pillar 01 — Governance

COBIT 2019

Provides governance objectives, performance metrics, and risk mitigation strategies that align IT with business value creation and regulatory compliance (Heruatmadja et al., 2023). Particularly suited to e-commerce maturity assessment and capability development.

☁️
Pillar 02 — Disaster Recovery

Cloud Hybrid DR Strategy

Geo-redundancy, automated failover, real-time backups, and system monitoring (Microsoft, 2024; Nur, 2025). Business continuity testing and role-based access controls ensure the plan remains both operative and secure under adversarial conditions.

⚖️
Pillar 03 — Compliance & Ethics

GDPR · PCI DSS · ACM

Legal accountability via GDPR data protection and PCI DSS payment security (Chippagiri and Ramesh, 2025; Otieno, 2025), underpinned by the ACM Code of Ethics for responsible AI and professional integrity (ACM, 2018).

Governance Framework — COBIT 2019

COBIT 2019
Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies
COBIT 2019 provides a governance and management framework built around 40 governance and management objectives, organised across five domains: Evaluate, Direct and Monitor (EDM); Align, Plan and Organise (APO); Build, Acquire and Implement (BAI); Deliver, Service and Support (DSS); and Monitor, Evaluate and Assess (MEA). For a global e-commerce company, COBIT's performance measurement system and risk management objectives enable the board to set IT risk appetite, monitor compliance posture, and align digital investment with commercial strategy (Heruatmadja et al., 2023).

Disaster Recovery Strategy Components

🌍
Component 01 · Infrastructure

Geo-Redundancy

Platform components deployed across multiple geographic regions. A regional outage triggers automatic traffic rerouting, ensuring service continuity for global customers (Microsoft, 2024).

🔀
Component 02 · Recovery

Automated Failover

Intelligent failover orchestration minimises manual intervention during disruption, reducing recovery time and human error in time-critical scenarios (Nur, 2025).

💾
Component 03 · Backup

Real-Time Backups

Continuous backup pipelines ensure near-zero recovery point objectives. Cloud-based backup strategies support geographically distributed data protection (Suguna and Suhasini, 2015).

📡
Component 04 · Monitoring

System Monitoring

Real-time monitoring and alerting across infrastructure layers enables early detection of anomalies, performance degradation, or security incidents before they escalate.

🧪
Component 05 · Testing

Business Continuity Testing

Regular DR drills and tabletop exercises validate the recovery plan under simulated conditions, ensuring staff readiness and plan currency (Tatineni, 2023).

🔑
Component 06 · Access Control

Role-Based Access Controls

Least-privilege access policies restrict recovery system permissions to authorised personnel only — preventing insider exploitation during a disruption event.

Ethical Implications of AI in Risk Management

🔍

Transparency

AI fraud detection systems must be auditable — customers and regulators have a legitimate interest in understanding why transactions are flagged or blocked. Opaque systems risk both ethical failure and regulatory challenge.

⚖️

Fairness & Non-Bias

AI models trained on historical transaction data may encode discriminatory patterns. Regular bias audits and diverse training data are essential to ensure fraud detection does not systematically disadvantage specific customer groups.

🛡️

Privacy Preservation

Data handled carelessly or AI systems operating without transparency can damage trust, breach ethical responsibilities, and risk non-compliance. Privacy by design must be embedded into AI system architecture from the outset (ACM, 2018).

Full Report

Identified Risk and Strategic Opportunity

A significant risk for a global e-commerce organisation is cybersecurity breaches, potentially leading to customer data exposure, financial loss, and reputational damage. However, a strategic opportunity exists in deploying AI-driven fraud detection and threat analysis tools, which can enhance transaction security and improve customer confidence in e-commerce environments.

Governance and Risk Mitigation Framework

To align IT with business goals, the company adopts the COBIT 2019 framework, which provides governance objectives, performance metrics, and risk mitigation strategies that support value creation and compliance (Heruatmadja et al., 2023). For disaster recovery (DR), the firm implements a cloud-based hybrid solution, integrating geo-redundancy, automated failover, real-time backups, and system monitoring (Microsoft, 2024; Nur, 2025; Suguna and Suhasini, 2015; Tatineni, 2023).

The plan includes business continuity testing and role-based access controls. The organisation ensures compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) for data protection and the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), promoting secure data handling and legal accountability (Chippagiri and Ramesh, 2025; Otieno, 2025).

Disaster Recovery Strategy

The organisation's disaster recovery approach focuses on maintaining operational continuity during cyberattacks, infrastructure failures, or large-scale service disruptions. Geo-redundancy ensures workloads are distributed across multiple geographic regions, reducing dependency on a single data centre or cloud region.

Automated failover systems minimise recovery time by shifting workloads to secondary infrastructure without requiring extensive manual intervention. Real-time backups and continuous replication support near-zero Recovery Point Objectives (RPOs), while monitoring systems provide early detection of abnormal activity, enabling rapid incident response.

Ethical Implications

The ethical use of AI in risk management demands transparency, fairness, and privacy-preserving practices. Legal and technical experts are guided by the ACM Code of Ethics, emphasising integrity, competence, and respect for user rights (Association for Computing Machinery, 2018).

If data is handled carelessly or AI systems operate without transparency, it can damage trust, breach ethical responsibilities, and risk non-compliance with regulations. Bias in AI-driven fraud detection systems may also unfairly affect legitimate customers, making regular auditing and explainability essential.

Benefits

Integrating COBIT 2019 governance practices with AI-supported cybersecurity and cloud-based disaster recovery improves organisational resilience, strengthens regulatory compliance, and enhances customer trust. Automated monitoring and failover reduce downtime, while structured governance improves strategic alignment between security investments and business objectives.

Challenges

Despite these advantages, implementing advanced governance and AI-driven security systems introduces operational and ethical challenges. AI systems require high-quality training data and continuous monitoring to avoid false positives and discriminatory outcomes. Multi-region cloud DR architectures also increase infrastructure complexity and operational costs, requiring skilled personnel and rigorous testing to ensure reliability.

Conclusion

By integrating IT governance with proactive risk management, the organisation reinforces operational resilience and stakeholder confidence in the digital economy. The combination of COBIT 2019, a cloud-based DR strategy, and ethical AI deployment positions the company to treat cybersecurity not as a cost centre but as a competitive differentiator.

Reflection

Why COBIT 2019 for this scenario?

COBIT 2019 was chosen because the scenario focuses on governance, compliance, and strategic risk management rather than operational IT service delivery. Unlike ITIL, which concentrates on service management processes, COBIT provides a governance-oriented structure that aligns cybersecurity, compliance, and business objectives. For a global e-commerce organisation handling customer transactions and sensitive payment data, governance visibility and accountability were more central to the problem than day-to-day operational workflows.

What did the AI fraud detection focus add?

Including AI-driven fraud detection shifted the analysis beyond traditional cybersecurity controls into questions of ethics, explainability, and trust. It highlighted that security technologies are not value-neutral — poorly designed AI systems can create unfair outcomes, false positives, or opaque decision making that damages customer trust. This made governance and ethical oversight feel as important as technical effectiveness.

How did the global e-commerce context change the analysis?

A global e-commerce environment introduced challenges around cross-border compliance, payment security, and always-on service availability. Unlike smaller or localised systems, downtime in a global platform affects customers across multiple jurisdictions simultaneously, increasing both reputational and legal consequences. It also reinforced how disaster recovery is not only a technical requirement but a business continuity necessity tied directly to customer confidence and revenue generation.

What would I extend in future work?

In future work, I would develop a quantitative risk assessment model incorporating Recovery Time Objectives (RTOs), Recovery Point Objectives (RPOs), and financial impact estimates for major disruptions. I would also explore governance mechanisms for auditing AI decision-making processes, particularly around explainability and bias detection, as these are increasingly important concerns in AI-supported cybersecurity systems.

Blog Post · Generative AI

Future Trends: How Generative AI Will Reshape Business and Society

Emerging Technology

"Foundation models aren't just a new tech trend — they're the start of a bigger shift in how we work, create and communicate."

In the last couple of years, tools like ChatGPT, Midjourney, and GitHub Copilot have gone from fun novelties to everyday work companions. At the heart of these tools are foundation models — huge AI systems trained on massive datasets that can perform a wide range of tasks, from writing code to generating images. What makes them special is that unlike earlier AI, which had to be trained for one specific task, these models can adapt to many jobs with just a few examples, or none at all. This flexibility is already having a significant impact on how businesses operate.

Right now, companies use them to speed up tasks like writing emails, generating images, summarising customer feedback, or helping maintain code. According to Stanford researchers, this "task-agnostic" approach is changing how firms think about workflows and automation (Bommasani et al., 2021). So what's next? Over the coming years, we'll likely see foundation models embedded in almost every part of business — from customer service bots that sound human to AI systems helping doctors interpret scans or lawyers draft contracts.

A study from the National Bureau of Economic Research found that when companies in Denmark started using large language models, workers at all skill levels began using automation — not just tech teams (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025). That's a huge shift. This growing use of AI won't just change tasks. It will likely change how teams are built and what skills are valued. In the future, we may see more hybrid roles that blend human creativity with AI capabilities — such as prompt engineers, AI strategists, or even "human-AI collaboration managers". For many jobs, knowing how to work alongside these tools could become just as important as technical expertise itself.

McKinsey researchers estimate that the global economic impact of generative AI could reach as much as $4.4 trillion per year, as it reshapes industries and enhances productivity across sectors (Chui et al., 2023). Industries like retail, education, law, and healthcare will be transformed — not just by doing things faster, but by changing how work itself is defined.

However, there are real risks involved. These models can "hallucinate" facts, reflect bias in the data they're trained on, or be misused to spread misinformation. Researchers have warned that we need stronger guardrails and better transparency (Dwivedi, Ghosh and Dwivedi, 2023). As AI becomes more powerful, society will need to catch up with new rules, new ethics, and new kinds of jobs. The bottom line is that foundation models aren't just a new tech trend — they're the start of a bigger shift in how we work, create, and communicate. And we're only just getting started.

Reflection on this Artefact

Why Generative AI over Blockchain or Quantum?

The assignment offered three options — AI, Blockchain, or Quantum Computing. Generative AI was the most compelling choice because it is the only one already having a measurable, observable effect on the labour market right now. Blockchain and Quantum Computing are significant in their respective domains, but their societal impact remains more speculative. The Denmark LLM study (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025) offered something rare in emerging-technology writing: actual labour market data from a natural experiment, which grounded the discussion in evidence rather than projection.

What did the blog format demand differently?

Writing in a blog style — accessible, conversational, with a clear narrative arc — required a different discipline from the report-writing I had practised in earlier units. The challenge was to maintain academic credibility (citing four peer-reviewed or institutional sources) while writing sentences that a non-specialist reader could follow without effort. The drop cap, the pull quote, and the sidebar statistics on this page reflect that dual register: the layout communicates "editorial" while the citations communicate "academic".

What would I develop further?

The blog post identifies the hallucination and bias risks at a high level but does not propose concrete technical mitigations. A longer-form extension would explore Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) as an architectural approach to reducing hallucination, and Constitutional AI as a framework for embedding value alignment into model training — both of which are active research areas directly relevant to the responsible deployment concerns raised in the final paragraph.
