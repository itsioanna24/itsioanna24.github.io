Fake News Detection
with Logistic Regression

A binary text classifier trained on ~45,000 labelled news articles using TF-IDF vectorisation and Logistic Regression, achieving 98.27% accuracy.

ML Assignment

The Problem

Misinformation in the media is a growing challenge with significant societal consequences. This project applies supervised machine learning to automatically classify news articles as either fake (0) or real (1), using a publicly available dataset of nearly 45,000 labelled articles. The model is built with Python's Scikit-learn library, using TF-IDF for text representation and Logistic Regression as the classifier.

Ethical Considerations

High accuracy does not guarantee responsible deployment. Three core ethical concerns apply to this model and to fake news detection systems more broadly.

⚖️

Dataset Bias

If certain sources or political perspectives are over-represented in the training data, the model may incorrectly label genuine content as fake. Balanced, diverse training corpora are essential to mitigate systematic bias (P, 2020).

🔍

Transparency

Logistic Regression is interpretable by design — feature weights can be inspected to understand which words drive predictions. More opaque models (deep neural networks) would demand additional explainability tooling to meet responsible AI standards.

🧑‍⚖️

Human Oversight

Automated classification at scale risks suppressing legitimate dissenting or minority viewpoints. Human review of flagged content, particularly near the decision boundary, is essential before any real-world deployment.

Model Limitations

01

Static vocabularyThe TF-IDF vocabulary is fixed at training time. New terminology, slang, or evolving misinformation tactics introduced after training will be poorly represented.

02

No semantic understandingTF-IDF captures word frequency, not meaning. Sarcasm, irony, or contextually misleading text with familiar vocabulary may be misclassified.

03

Dataset domainThe model was trained on a specific corpus of English-language articles. Performance on other languages, domains, or publication formats is unknown.

04

Binary framingThe fake/real label is a simplification. Real-world misinformation exists on a spectrum — satire, misleading framing, and selective omission sit between these two poles.

Reflection

What did I learn from the NLP pipeline?

Before this assignment I had only encountered TF-IDF as a formula. Implementing it as a TfidfVectorizer step in a pipeline — watching raw text become a sparse numeric matrix — made the transformation concrete. The max_df=0.7 parameter was a practical decision, filtering out words appearing in over 70% of documents as they carry little discriminatory signal. Understanding why each hyperparameter exists deepened my engagement with the method significantly.

What did the metrics tell me beyond accuracy?

Accuracy of 98.27% is impressive, but the precision and recall figures are more informative for this task. A false positive — real journalism flagged as fake — has different real-world consequences from a false negative — misinformation classified as real. The model's near-equal precision and recall suggest it is not biased toward one error type, which is a meaningful design quality for a content-moderation use case.

How did this change my view of "high accuracy"?

Writing the ethics section was the most challenging part of the assignment and the most valuable. A model with 98% accuracy on a benchmark dataset can still be systematically unfair if the benchmark itself encodes bias. This gap between statistical performance and real-world reliability is something I had previously underappreciated, and it has reshaped how I will evaluate AI systems going forward.

What would I extend in future work?

I would compare Logistic Regression against a transformer-based model such as DistilBERT, which captures semantic relationships that TF-IDF cannot. I would also apply LIME or SHAP to explain individual predictions, making the model's reasoning auditable — a prerequisite for responsible deployment in any content moderation context.
