For each threat, mitigations grounded in current literature are proposed.

Threat Landscape

Cybersecurity threats operate at multiple levels — from human psychology to model architecture. The four primary threats examined span both conventional and AI-specific attack surfaces.

🎣
Traditional · Human Layer

Phishing

Deceptive communications — fake emails, messages, or calls — manipulate individuals into revealing credentials or financial data. Variants include spear-phishing (targeted) and vishing (voice-based). Exploits psychological rather than technical vulnerabilities (Desolda et al., 2022).

🔓
Traditional · Data Layer

Data Breaches

Unauthorised access to or exposure of confidential information. Causes include external cyberattacks, insider misuse, and negligence. Consequences range from financial and reputational damage to legal liability. Successful phishing is a frequent entry point (Molitor et al., 2024).

☠️
AI-Specific · Training Time

Data Poisoning

Attackers corrupt training data or model architecture to degrade performance, cause targeted misclassification, or affect specific subpopulations. In graph systems, malicious nodes manipulate classification and recommendation outputs (Fan et al., 2022).

🐴
AI-Specific · Model Layer

Trojan & Reprogramming

Trojan attacks embed hidden backdoors directly into model weights, triggered later to hijack or degrade behaviour. Model reprogramming repurposes a model for entirely unintended tasks via minor triggers, without requiring data access (Sotiropoulos, 2024).

AI Attack Taxonomy

Adversarial attacks occur at two distinct stages of the model lifecycle, each requiring different defensive approaches.

🧪

Availability Attacks

Raise overall test error rates, degrading the model's general usefulness. Most detectable via standard performance monitoring.

🎯

Targeted Misclassification

Stealthy attacks that mislead classifiers about specific inputs while maintaining normal accuracy elsewhere — harder to detect by routine evaluation.

🕳️

Backdoor Triggers

Malicious behaviours embedded silently into model weights, remaining dormant until a specific input pattern or trigger is applied at inference time.

Full Report

Introduction

The reliance on digital systems grows across all sectors, and the importance of securing them becomes increasingly critical. Traditional risks such as phishing and data breaches remain persistent, while new vulnerabilities emerge with the advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI). Cybersecurity threats target systems at multiple levels — from human error to infrastructure vulnerabilities. This report outlines key threats and recommends effective security measures to mitigate the risks.

Phishing & Data Breaches

Among the various cybersecurity challenges, phishing and data breaches remain two of the most prevalent and damaging vulnerabilities. Phishing involves deceptive tactics — fake emails, messages, or calls — that manipulate individuals into revealing sensitive data such as passwords or financial details (Desolda et al., 2022). These attacks exploit psychological vulnerabilities and can take various forms, including spear-phishing or voice-based "vishing" (Desolda et al., 2022). Successful phishing can lead directly to data breaches, which occur when unauthorised parties access or expose confidential information (Molitor et al., 2024). A breach may result from cyberattacks, insider misuse, or negligence, and its consequences range from financial loss to reputational damage (Molitor et al., 2024). Such breaches underscore the critical need for robust security protocols and accountability mechanisms within organisations.

Adversarial Attacks on AI Systems

Adversarial attacks on AI systems can occur during training or deployment and include techniques such as data poisoning, Trojan horse attacks, and model reprogramming. Data poisoning targets training data or architecture to degrade model performance, cause targeted misclassification, or affect specific subpopulations without impacting overall accuracy (Fan et al., 2022). Attackers may use availability attacks to raise test error rates, or stealthy targeted attacks to mislead classifiers about specific inputs. In graph-based systems, malicious nodes can be inserted to manipulate node classification and recommendation algorithms (Fan et al., 2022).

In contrast, Trojan horse attacks embed malicious behaviours directly into the model without modifying data. These backdoors can later be triggered to hijack the model or degrade performance (Sotiropoulos, 2024). Model reprogramming extends this threat by enabling attackers to repurpose a model for entirely unintended tasks, often using only minor inputs or triggers (Sotiropoulos, 2024), demonstrating the growing sophistication of AI-targeted threats.

Mitigations

To mitigate cybersecurity threats, several robust measures can be implemented. Utilising machine learning techniques — such as graph inference and logistic regression — can effectively detect and prevent phishing attacks (Naqvi et al., 2023). Implementing endpoint security measures, including updated antivirus software, malware protection, and host-based intrusion detection systems (HIDS), enhances defence against malicious activities (Naqvi et al., 2023). To counteract data poisoning attacks, majority vote mechanisms have been proposed, where multiple base classifiers trained on different data subsets are combined to produce a robust aggregate model (Goldblum et al., 2022). Furthermore, implementing Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) greatly lowers the chance of unauthorised access by requiring users to verify their identity through more than one method (Nagaraju Pureti, 2020). Research indicates that MFA can reduce the likelihood of account breaches by more than 99.9% (Wahab et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Cyber threats in computing are diverse, evolving from traditional phishing and data breaches to more sophisticated attacks targeting AI systems. Addressing risks such as data poisoning and Trojan horse attacks requires a layered defence strategy combining technical controls, human awareness, and continuous model monitoring.

Reflection

What was new to me in this topic?

I was familiar with phishing and data breaches at a conceptual level, but the taxonomy of adversarial AI attacks — particularly the distinction between data poisoning (training-time) and Trojan/reprogramming attacks (model-level) — was largely new. The idea that an attacker can embed a backdoor into model weights without touching the training data significantly changed how I think about ML model supply chains and third-party model use.

What connection did I find most striking?

The link between phishing and data breaches is intuitive, but researching ML-based phishing detection — specifically that logistic regression, the same algorithm used in the previous unit's fake news classifier, is applied to detect phishing — made the cross-unit connection concrete. The same technique that can be weaponised (adversarial ML) can also defend against conventional threats, illustrating how dual-use machine learning truly is.

What challenged me in writing?

Synthesising sources across very different attack categories — human-layer social engineering, infrastructure-level breaches, and model-level adversarial attacks — into a coherent layered defence narrative required careful structural thinking. I initially treated each threat in isolation, but the revision process pushed me towards a more integrated argument: that no single control is sufficient and a layered strategy is essential.

What would I extend in future?

I would investigate certified defences against data poisoning more deeply — particularly randomised smoothing and differential privacy techniques, which offer provable robustness guarantees rather than heuristic ones. I would also explore how the EU AI Act imposes specific security obligations on AI system providers, situating these technical mitigations within a legal and governance framework.
