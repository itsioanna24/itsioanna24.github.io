The Scenario

Hypothetical E-Commerce Enterprise

Europe & Asia Operations Cloud Infrastructure Third-Party Plugin Dependencies

A mid-sized e-commerce enterprise recently suffered attacks that disrupted transaction processing. The company handles customer financial data, utilises third-party plugins, and relies on cloud infrastructure for data storage and payment processing — creating a broad and interconnected attack surface requiring a structured, layered security response.

Threat Assessment — Identified Vulnerabilities

Four distinct vulnerability categories were identified, spanning supply chain risk, human factors, application-layer weaknesses, and cloud misconfiguration.

💣
Critical · Supply Chain

Ransomware via Outdated Plugin

A ransomware attack exploited an outdated third-party plugin, revealing absent patch management and inadequate controls for external software. Third-party plugins are a common malware entry vector when update cycles are neglected (ENISA, 2023).

📧
High · Human Factor

Phishing & Credential Theft

Phishing emails targeted customer service staff, successfully extracting login credentials. This indicates inadequate authentication mechanisms and insufficient security awareness training (Khadka et al., 2024).

💉
High · Application Layer

XSS & SQL Injection

The platform is exposed to cross-site scripting (XSS) and SQL injection — two of the most frequently exploited vulnerabilities in poorly secured web interfaces (Rodríguez et al., 2020).

☁️
Medium · Cloud Infrastructure

Cloud Misconfiguration

Cloud services used for data storage and payment processing pose risks if access settings are improperly configured or if customer data is stored in jurisdictions with weak privacy protections (Lynn et al., 2021).

Compliance Considerations

Regulation

GDPR

Mandates data minimisation, secure processing, and breach notification within 72 hours. As an enterprise operating across Europe, GDPR compliance is a legal requirement and a baseline for customer trust (European Union, 2016).

Standard

PCI DSS

Required for any enterprise processing payment card data. Mandates encrypted transmission, access controls, and regular security testing to protect cardholder data and reduce fraud risk (Rahaman et al., 2019).

Framework

ISO/IEC 27001

Provides a risk-based information security management framework with structured controls and regular audit requirements. Supports access control, incident management, and continuous improvement (ISO, 2022).

Ethical Considerations

Beyond legal compliance, the enterprise carries ethical obligations to its users — particularly around transparency and responsible data handling in cloud environments where privacy expectations are easily undermined.

⚖️

Principlist Framework

Cybersecurity practice should follow the principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability — requiring the enterprise to affirmatively protect users, avoid harm, and ensure transparency in data handling (Formosa et al., 2021).

Transparency & Trust

Full Report

Enterprise Context

This report examines a hypothetical mid-sized e-commerce enterprise operating across Europe and Asia that recently suffered attacks that disrupted transaction processing. The company handles customer financial data, utilises third-party plugins, and relies on cloud infrastructure.

Threat Assessment

The enterprise is exposed to several cybersecurity threats. Firstly, a ransomware attack exploited an outdated third-party plugin, revealing that the company lacked proper updates and controls for external software — a common malware entry vector (ENISA, 2023). Secondly, phishing emails were sent to customer service staff and successfully tricked them into revealing login credentials, indicating inadequate use of authentication mechanisms (Khadka et al., 2024). Thirdly, the platform is exposed to application-level threats including cross-site scripting (XSS) and SQL injection (Rodríguez et al., 2020), which are frequently exploited by attackers targeting poorly secured web interfaces. The enterprise also uses cloud services for data storage and payment processing, which is risky if access settings are not properly configured or if customer data is stored in countries with weak privacy laws (Lynn et al., 2021).

Mitigation Plan

To mitigate these risks, a multi-layered security strategy is essential. The most important proposition is the implementation of multi-factor authentication (MFA) for all administrative and customer accounts, which would reduce the likelihood of credential misuse. Research by Meyer et al. (2023) revealed that MFA can prevent over 99.99% of unauthorised access attempts, and even when credentials were leaked, MFA reduced compromise risk by 98.56%. A web application firewall (WAF) should be deployed to filter malicious traffic and prevent XSS and SQL injection attacks (Dawadi et al., 2023). Regular patch management must be enforced, especially for third-party plugins, to reduce exploitation risk (Dissanayake et al., 2021). For threat detection and rapid response, endpoint detection and response (EDR) tools should be adopted to monitor and isolate malware activity in real time (Arfeen et al., 2021).

Protecting sensitive data requires encryption during transmission and at rest. TLS secures data during transmission (Zhou et al., 2024), while Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is widely used for protecting stored data, particularly in cloud environments (Fatima et al., 2022). Access controls must be enforced, and compliance with ISO/IEC 27001 supports this through risk-based security management and regular audits (ISO, 2022).

Legal Compliance

The enterprise is required to align with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which mandates data minimisation, breach notification within 72 hours, and secure processing (European Union, 2016). Compliance with PCI DSS is essential as it protects cardholder data, reducing the risk of fraud and data breaches (Rahaman et al., 2019).

Ethical Considerations

From an ethical perspective, the enterprise is obliged to protect user data in accordance with principles of privacy and transparency. These practices should follow ethical principles — beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability — requiring the enterprise to affirmatively protect users, avoid harm, and ensure transparency in data handling (Formosa et al., 2021). Transparency about breaches and responsible data handling are essential to maintaining user trust and respecting digital rights, particularly in cloud storage contexts where privacy expectations may be undermined (Loi and Christen, 2020).

Conclusion

By adopting a layered defence strategy and adhering to legal and ethical standards, the enterprise can significantly enhance its cybersecurity defences and build trust with stakeholders.

Reflection

What did the scenario format add?

Grounding the analysis in a concrete hypothetical enterprise made the threat assessment significantly more focused than a generic overview. Having to think about a company that uses third-party plugins, cloud infrastructure, and cross-border data transfers forced me to consider how vulnerabilities interact — for instance, how an unpatched plugin creates the entry point that ransomware then exploits, which then triggers GDPR breach notification obligations.

What surprised me about the MFA evidence?

The empirical strength of MFA was striking. A 99.99% reduction in unauthorised access, and a 98.56% risk reduction even when credentials are already compromised (Meyer et al., 2023), is a remarkably strong result for a single control. This reframed MFA for me — not as a supplementary measure but as arguably the single highest-return security investment available to any enterprise.

How did compliance and ethics interact?

I initially treated GDPR, PCI DSS, and ISO/IEC 27001 as separate compliance checkboxes. Working through the report made clear they are mutually reinforcing: ISO 27001 provides the management framework, GDPR mandates the data minimisation and incident response obligations, and PCI DSS addresses the specific payment context. The principlist ethical framework from Formosa et al. (2021) then sits above all three — articulating the human obligations that regulation alone cannot fully capture.

What would I extend in future work?

I would develop a formal risk register using a quantitative framework such as FAIR (Factor Analysis of Information Risk) to assign probability and financial impact estimates to each vulnerability. I would also explore the specific cloud security challenges introduced by operating across European and Asian jurisdictions — where data residency requirements and cross-border transfer rules differ significantly — as this is a real compliance gap for many e-commerce operators.
