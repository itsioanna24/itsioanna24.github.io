Strengths & Weaknesses

✓  Strengths
  • GNNs and hybrid deep learning improve predictive accuracy under class imbalance and non-stationary conditions (Singh et al., 2024; Sha et al., 2025)
  • RAG-backed chatbots reduce hallucination and improve resolution consistency (Pattnayak et al., 2025)
  • Federated learning enables fraud models to benefit from cross-region data while maintaining GDPR compliance (Abadi et al., 2024)
  • SHAP and XAI tools preserve regulatory auditability across all three applications
!  Weaknesses
  • GNNs and transformers are computationally expensive — real-time latency is a genuine constraint (Sarkar et al., 2022)
  • Bias risk: models trained on historical financial data may replicate societal inequalities (Mariscal et al., 2024)
  • Deep models are less transparent to regulators — ongoing XAI investment required (Bücker et al., 2020)
  • Diverse alternative data sources raise consent, privacy and security challenges under GDPR

Implementation Challenges

⚡

Latency & Computational Cost

GNN and transformer architectures place significant strain on inference latency. Real-time financial predictions require low-latency feature pipelines and optimised model serving (Phillips et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025).

🛡️

Data Privacy & GDPR

User consent, pseudonymisation, federated learning and differential privacy are required mechanisms. Each introduces substantial engineering and compliance costs (Byrd and Polychroniadou, 2020; Yang, 2025).

🤝

Human-in-the-Loop Requirements

Overreliance on automation undermines customer trust. Maintaining human fallback processes is essential — particularly for credit decisions subject to GDPR Article 22 (European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025).

⚖️

Regulatory Risk

Jurisdictions differ in acceptance of black-box models. Financial regulators increasingly demand interpretability and auditability — misalignment risks penalties and reputational damage (Bücker et al., 2020).

Full Report

1. Introduction

The financial technology sector is rapidly changing as Artificial Intelligence (AI) becomes integral to business operations. Firms leveraging AI gain efficiency, reduce risks, and enhance customer trust (Cao, 2023). For digital-first companies like Revolut, competitiveness depends on integrating AI responsibly, balancing innovation with security and compliance. Leadership recognises AI's potential but remains cautious about bias, data security, and reputational risks (Ahmed, 2024). This report explores three key AI applications for Revolut and examines the data, methods, and challenges involved.

2. Company and Industry Context

Our company operates as a digital-first financial services platform, offering payments, foreign exchange, savings, investments, and emerging credit products across more than 30 markets (Revolut, 2024a). The fintech industry is characterised by low-margin, high-volume transactions, stringent regulatory requirements, and customer expectations of instant, seamless service. Competitors such as N26 and Monzo have grown quickly, but differentiation increasingly hinges on data-driven services rather than core banking functionality (Foy and Arnold, 2025).

Internally, Revolut is structured around modular engineering teams that deliver functionality via microservices on Google Cloud Platform (GCP). Data science is a core capability: Revolut employs over 45 data scientists and more than 100 Python engineers, working on fraud detection, NLP, and computer vision for ID verification (Butcher, 2022). Publicly, we have launched an AI-scam detection feature that reduced fraud losses in targeted categories by 30% in its first months of deployment (Reynolds, 2024; Revolut, 2024b).

3. Key AI Applications

3.1 Automated Credit Risk Assessment

Our current credit scoring stack uses ensemble tree models (LightGBM / XGBoost) combined with transaction history and static bureau scores. Recent studies reveal that hybrid and graph-based architectures yield better predictive power under class imbalance, non-stationary data, and when incorporating non-traditional features (Bitetto et al., 2024). This proposal suggests maintaining tree-ensemble models for baseline performance, but integrating a Graph Neural Network (GNN) component to model relationships among borrowers (Sun et al., 2024), and piloting narrative data extraction using transformer-based models such as fine-tuned Llama2 or GPT-derived embedding extractors (Das et al., 2023; Tavakoli et al., 2025). Focal Loss addresses class imbalance (Xia et al., 2024), while SHAP provides the explainability required for regulatory compliance (Lundberg and Lee, 2017; Rudin, 2019). Model evaluation must include temporal cross-validation, macroeconomic stress scenarios, and fairness audits covering demographic parity and equalised odds (Gambacorta et al., 2024).

3.2 Customer Service Chatbots

The present chatbot system supports structured flows and keyword triggers. To improve user satisfaction in ambiguous or emotionally charged interactions, a transformer-based architecture using multilingual XML-R or fine-tuned BERT should form the backbone of intent classification (Devlin et al., 2018; Pandhare, 2024). Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) frameworks using a curated knowledge base will ensure factual and consistent replies rather than free-generation that risks hallucination (Lewis et al., 2021). Sentiment and emotion detection modules trained on labelled transcripts can detect frustration or urgency (Chintala, 2024; DeZao, 2024), with automatic escalation to human agents. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) optimises the chatbot's policy over time (Christiano et al., 2017).

3.3 Fraud Detection and Prevention

Sophisticated fraud attacks require a multi-modal defence: supervised classifiers for known patterns, autoencoder and isolation forest anomaly detection for novel behaviour (Talukder et al., 2024), and GNNs for relational fraud through transaction networks (Sha et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024). Federated learning setups allow fraud detection models to benefit from cross-region data while maintaining user privacy (Umakor et al., 2025), with differential privacy and secure multi-party computation ensuring regulatory compliance (Pentyala et al., 2022). Streaming pipelines via Kafka feed low-latency scoring endpoints, while human review loops suppress false positives (Singh et al., 2024).

4. Data Requirements and Development Approaches

Enhanced models require more diverse, higher-resolution data. MLOps practices — version control, CI/CD, rollback capability, drift detection, and automated alerting — must be standard across all three applications (Liang et al., 2024). Model training must follow robust validation protocols: temporal validation, cross-geographic validation, stress simulation, and fairness audits (Černevičienė and Kabašinskas, 2024).

5. Potential Challenges in Implementation

Key technical challenges include the increased model complexity of GNN and transformer architectures, which strain latency and computational resources (Phillips et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025). Data privacy requires mechanisms such as user consent, pseudonymisation, federated learning, and differential privacy — each introducing substantial engineering costs (Byrd and Polychroniadou, 2020; Yang, 2025). Overreliance on automation risks undermining trust, reinforcing the importance of human-in-the-loop fallback processes (European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025). Finally, jurisdictional variation in acceptance of black-box models creates regulatory risk if interpretability and auditability are not maintained (Bücker et al., 2020).

6. Conclusion

Revolut is well-positioned to advance AI capabilities across credit risk, customer service, and fraud detection. By leveraging existing infrastructure, hybrid models, graph neural networks, and privacy-preserving methods, the company can improve predictive accuracy, customer satisfaction, and fraud prevention. Responsible deployment with monitoring, fairness audits, and human oversight will ensure compliance and maintain trust. Success will depend on effectively managing fairness, regulatory requirements, and the costs of advanced AI infrastructure.

Reflection on this Artefact

How did the report evolve from the Unit 6 outline?

The most significant shift was moving from generic case studies (Zest AI, Bank of America Erica, PayPal) to a deep analysis of Revolut's actual architecture. Grounding the proposals in Revolut's GCP microservices, event-driven domain model, and existing ML team made the recommendations specific and actionable rather than advisory. The technical depth also increased substantially — Graph Neural Networks, RAG, RLHF, and federated learning were not in the outline, but emerged from reading the most current arXiv and ACM literature on each topic area.

What was the most challenging aspect to write?

Section 3.1 on credit risk was the most technically demanding. Synthesising recent literature on GNNs, transformer embeddings, Focal Loss, SHAP, and temporal cross-validation — while connecting each to Revolut's existing LightGBM/XGBoost stack — required understanding not just what each technique does, but why it would represent an improvement over the current system specifically. The fairness audit requirements across 30+ markets also introduced regulatory complexity that went beyond the outline's simple mention of GDPR.

What does the cold-start problem look like at Revolut specifically?

The Unit 6 outline identified the cold-start problem as a risk for a generic start-up. At Revolut, the challenge is different: the data exists (45+ data scientists, 100+ Python engineers, real-time fraud monitoring), but the challenge is data quality, consent infrastructure, and cross-jurisdictional governance. Open banking feeds require regulatory permissions that differ by market. Alternative behavioural data (device events, app usage) raises GDPR legitimate interest questions. The final report reflects this shift — the data challenge is less about volume and more about governance and legal basis.

What would I extend in future work?

I would develop a prioritisation matrix — scoring each proposed enhancement on implementation complexity, regulatory readiness, and expected business impact — to give leadership a sequenced roadmap rather than three parallel proposals. I would also explore the interaction between the federated fraud detection system and Revolut's FinCrime domain specifically, where cross-border data sharing has additional AML and sanctions screening implications beyond standard GDPR analysis.
