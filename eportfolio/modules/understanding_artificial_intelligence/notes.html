Unit 1 – Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

Lecturecast Notes

  • Definitions of AI tend to cluster around four thematic areas (Russell & Norvig, 2021): thinking humanly, acting humanly, thinking rationally, and acting rationally.
  • Arthur Lee Samuel defined machine learning as "a branch of AI which aims to equip computers with the ability to learn without being programmed."

Key Challenges Associated with AI

  • Learning algorithms: determining how algorithms should be trained and whether they are performing as expected.
  • Data quality: while vast amounts of data are generated, the quality of data used to develop an AI system is critical.
  • Cybersecurity: once deployed, especially in cloud-based or internet-connected environments, AI systems face significant security threats.

FAST Track Principles

PrincipleMeaning
FairnessAI systems should treat all users equitably and without discrimination.
AccountabilityResponsibility for AI decisions must be clearly assigned.
SustainabilityAI development must consider long-term societal and environmental impact.
TransparencyAI systems and their decision-making processes should be explainable.

EU AI Act — Banned Applications

  • Biometric categorisation systems using sensitive characteristics (political, religious, philosophical beliefs, sexual orientation, race).
  • Untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV to create facial recognition databases.
  • AI systems for emotional recognition in workplaces and educational institutions.
  • Social scoring based on social behaviour or personal characteristics.
  • Systems that manipulate human behaviour to circumvent free will.
  • AI systems that exploit vulnerabilities related to age, disability, or social/economic situation.

Unit 3 – Intelligent Agents and Learning Types

Lecturecast Notes

  • AI definitions cluster around two themes: thinking and acting rationally, and thinking and acting humanly.
  • Agent (Russell & Norvig, 2021): anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon it through actuators.
  • Environments can be single-agent (the agent operates independently) or multi-agent (agents interact with or depend on other agents).

Types of Agents

Agent TypeCharacteristic
Simple Reflex AgentActs solely based on current percept, ignoring history.
Model-based Reflex AgentMaintains an internal model of the world to handle partial observability.
Goal-based AgentActs to achieve a defined goal rather than just reacting.
Utility-based AgentChooses actions that maximise a utility function.
Learning AgentImproves its performance over time through experience.

Types of Learning from Feedback

TypeDescription
SupervisedAgent observes labelled input-output pairs and learns a mapping function.
Semi-supervisedA few labelled examples augmented with a large collection of unlabelled examples.
UnsupervisedAgent learns patterns in input data with no explicit feedback.
ReinforcementAgent learns from a series of rewards or punishments.

Supervised Learning Tasks

  • Classification: predicting a discrete output label.
  • Regression: predicting a continuous numerical value.

Unit 5 – Machine Learning Algorithms and Generative AI

Lecturecast Notes

Learning System Approaches

  • Supervised Approaches
  • Unsupervised Approaches

Decision Trees

  • Represents a function that takes a vector of attribute values and returns a single output ("decision"); values can be discrete or continuous.
  • Advantages: easy to read and explain; internal workings are observable; modest computing power required.
  • Limitations: can create overly complex models; some concepts are difficult to express in tree form.

Decision Tree Algorithm Types

AlgorithmFull Name
ID3Iterative Dichotomiser 3
C4.5Successor of ID3; handles continuous attributes
CHAIDChi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection
MARSMultivariate Adaptive Regression Splines

Supervised Machine Learning Algorithms

  • Association Rules Algorithms — commonly used in retail basket analysis.
  • Support Vector Machine (SVM) Algorithm
  • Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) Algorithms

Clustering

  • Organises objects that share similar characteristics into groups.
  • Ideal for unsupervised learning tasks where no prior training labels are available.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

  • Part of the neural network family with multiple layers, mostly used in Deep Learning tasks.
  • Form the backbone of most models that create novel output (randomly or from a user prompt).
  • Unlike supervised learning models, generative models do not produce labels.
  • Consist of two competing neural networks trained together:
    • Generator: learns how to create fake inputs for the discriminator.
    • Discriminator: learns to differentiate between fake and real inputs.

Unit 7 – Feature Engineering

Lecturecast Notes

  • Features are the building blocks of a learning system — they describe underlying objects and are useful for distinguishing and characterising different categories. They are important for producing accurate and interpretable learning systems.

Types of Features

TypeDescriptionExample
CategoricalSet of discrete values"colour" with values {black, blue, brown}
BinaryExactly two values"alive" with values {Yes, No}
OrdinalSet of ordered values"degree" with values {Bachelor, Master, PhD}
NumericalSet of numerical / continuous values"age" with integer values 1–50

Feature Engineering Processes

  • Feature Generation: constructing new features from existing ones using mathematical mapping (means, ratios, differences, logarithms, powers).
  • Feature Transformation: applying mathematical principles to change the value representation of an existing feature.
  • Feature Selection: selecting a small, optimal subset from a large pool of features — reduces computational cost and can improve result quality.
  • Feature Analysis and Evaluation: using concepts, methods, and measures to evaluate the usefulness of features and feature sets; often integrated into feature selection.

Feature Engineering Tasks

  • Scaling and Normalisation
  • Discretisation (Dichotomisation)
  • Standardisation
  • Decorrelation

Dimensionality Reduction Approaches

  • Linear Methods: Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Principal Component Analysis (PCA).
  • Non-linear Methods: Locally Linear Embedding, Kernel Learning, Neural network-based techniques.

Unit 9 – Evaluation of Learning Systems

Lecturecast Notes

  • Evaluation of learning systems is a measure of performance against data curated by human experts.

Evaluation Steps

  1. Feed training data to a learning algorithm so it learns patterns.
  2. Allow the algorithm to predict target classes or labels of a human-curated test set.
  3. Count wrong predictions to compute the learning algorithm's prediction accuracy.

Evaluation Methods

MethodDescription
Train-test Split Data divided into training and evaluation sets. The evaluation set is withheld from training until final assessment (the "unseen" test set).
K-fold Cross-Validation Data split into K subsets; each subset is used once as a test set while the rest train the model. Repeated K times.
Bootstrapping Employs a leave-one-out sampling strategy; test subset is randomly generated (bootstrapped) for each round.
Hybrid Methods Combines elements of the above; the test set is selected from within the training set and thus was part of training at some point.

Considerations for Method Selection

  • Size of the dataset
  • Distribution of target classes

Confusion Matrix

Predicted PositivePredicted Negative
Actual PositiveTrue Positive (TP)False Negative (FN)
Actual NegativeFalse Positive (FP)True Negative (TN)

Metrics Derived from the Confusion Matrix

  • Precision
  • Recall
  • Sensitivity
  • Specificity
  • Accuracy
  • F-measure
  • Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC)

Unit 11 – AI and the Future

Lecturecast Notes

  • The increasing rate of data generation means AI technologies will continue to thrive.
  • Digital life continues to enhance human capacities and disrupt established human activities.
  • AI learning systems continue to spread globally, reaching over half the world's population.
  • Opportunities exist to use AI in diagnosing and treating patients, helping the aged live fuller lives, and through public-health big data technologies capturing personal genomes, nutrition data, and more.
  • AI may offer unimaginable opportunities as well as unforeseeable threats to humans and society.

Mitigation Strategies

  • Global Good as the Topmost Priority: AI development should serve the collective benefit of humanity.
  • Values-based System: embed ethical values into the design and deployment of AI from the outset.
  • Prioritise People: keep human welfare and rights at the centre of AI decision-making.
